Two language models read the same {{fact:x.viewer.texts}} texts once, and every hidden state is stored. For each token we ask where its most similar tokens are, when similarity compares two tokens' recent pasts. Stage A (this site) counts where those similar tokens sit and checks whether they share the model's next-token predictions. Stage B has since been redesigned around independent chunks of text, one per document; it is written up and reviewed, but nothing of it has been run.

Why measure neighbours first

The long-term aim is a map of what a language model does to a text, layer by layer. Each token is a point. Two points are linked when the model's hidden states there are similar, and the map is the main patterns of that graph. The owner asked that similarity should include each token's recent past, not only its current state. Here that is called memory: how far back the comparison looks, with older positions counting less. A free pilot on a small model ({{fact:x.method.pilot.model}}) found that with memory, a token's nearest neighbours moved into its own text and closer to it. The median distance to same-text neighbours (one average removed, range over the pilot's three layers) was {{fact:x.method.pilot.gap.mem_none}} positions with no memory, {{fact:x.method.pilot.gap.mem_8}} at memory 8, {{fact:x.method.pilot.gap.mem_64}} at memory 64 and {{fact:x.method.pilot.gap.mem_whole}} at the whole past. A map built on such links might say little more than where in which text a token sits. So Stage A builds no map. It measures where neighbours are, and whether they share the model's next-token predictions, on two larger models and texts from the Pile.

The pilot used four Wikipedia articles, two Python standard-library files and two licence texts, {{fact:x.method.pilot.tokens}} tokens each. It looked at layers 3, 6 and 9, and at each token's 64 nearest neighbours from position {{fact:x.method.pilot.burn}} on. Whether a map built on such neighbours would be mostly about position within each text was not measured.

Memory 2 was added between none and 8 to fill the gap, not because of any finding.

Models and extraction

Nothing is trained. Two models of about a billion parameters, Pythia-1b and OLMo-1B, each read every text once. Their tokenizers give identical ids on every accepted text, and the run stops if they do not; only their end-of-text ids differ. From that one pass we keep, at every position, the hidden state after each of the 16 blocks and the model's 20 most likely next tokens. The analysis uses the states after blocks 2, 4, ..., 16. The main figures show layer 8, the state after 8 of the 16 blocks. Pythia's training held out the Pile test split; OLMo's training data may include these texts.

One forward pass per text and model in float32, eager attention, TF32 off. "Layer $k$" is the raw hidden state at the output of block $k$: after block $k$, before block $k+1$. Layer 16 is before the final normalisation and output head, and layer 0 is the output of the token embedding. On the first text, layers 1 to 15 were checked equal, bit for bit, to the library's own hidden states. A causal check confirms that changing the second half of a text leaves the first half's states unchanged (to one part in 100,000). Also stored per position: the top 20 next-token predictions with their probabilities under the full softmax (not renormalised over the 20), and per-head attention summaries (never the full attention). Texts that contain special or placeholder strings are rejected.

Texts and tokens

Each text is the first {{fact:x.viewer.text_tokens}} tokens of a long, mostly ASCII document from the Pile's test split. There are {{fact:x.viewer.texts}} texts from {{fact:x.method.probe.subsets}} Pile sources, at most four from each. Before each text, the model reads its own end-of-text token at position 0; that position is left out of every average and every comparison. The first {{fact:x.viewer.burn_in}} text tokens are part of later tokens' histories, but are never selected as queries or neighbours. That leaves {{fact:x.viewer.pool_tokens}} analysed tokens, from position {{fact:x.viewer.first_position}} to the end of each text. Fixed samples of them are the queries for the prediction check, the graph-weight test and the audit.

The texts are {{fact:x.method.probe.description}}. A document in the Pile's test file was eligible if it contains no special or placeholder strings, is at least 95% ASCII and is at least 2,048 tokens long. The eligible documents were shuffled with a fixed seed and taken in that order, keeping the first {{fact:x.viewer.text_tokens}} tokens of each. A document was skipped if more than 30% of those tokens are whitespace only, if more than 10% of its 32-token passages repeat an earlier one, if it shares a 32-token passage with an accepted text, or if its source already has four accepted texts. {{fact:x.method.probe.texts}} texts from {{fact:x.method.probe.subsets}} sources were accepted. Four more documents, in two pairs from different sources, make the {{fact:x.viewer.stitched}} spliced texts (see Controls).

A token is named by its text and position. Positions 1 to {{fact:x.viewer.burn_in}} are read by the model, count in the averages that are removed, and are part of every later token's past, but they are never queries or candidate neighbours.

Similarity

Every result here depends on how the similarity of two tokens is defined. Four choices set it: how far back into each token's past the comparison reaches (memory); whether vectors are compared by angle (cosine) or by the distance between histories with vector lengths kept (the "dot" setting); which average is removed, and whether some tokens are barred as neighbours; and how exact ties are broken. The same comparison is also run on text-only inputs, vectors that know only which token is where, to show what the token sequence and the formula give by themselves.

Memory: two pasts lined up at their ends

To compare token $t$ with token $t'$, line up their pasts so that the two tokens sit at the right-hand end. Compare $t$ with $t'$, then $t-1$ with $t'-1$, then $t-2$ with $t'-2$, and so on, and add up these step similarities with weights that fade going back. Memory $m$ sets how fast they fade: roughly, the last $m$ tokens count. "None" compares only the two current states, each of which already reflects earlier text through attention. "Whole" weights every earlier position equally. Each step pairs two positions the same distance apart, so two adjacent tokens are not similar merely because their pasts overlap.

With $y_u$ the hidden state at position $u$ after the average is removed (and, when comparing by angle, scaled to unit length; $y_u = 0$ before position 1), the similarity of tokens $t$ and $t'$ is:

$$K_a(t,t') = \sum_{s \ge 0} a^s\, \langle y_{t-s},\, y_{t'-s} \rangle = \langle y_t, y_{t'} \rangle + a\, K_a(t-1,\, t'-1).$$

Memory $m$ sets $a = 1 - 1/m$: none ($a = 0$, the current token only), 2 ($a = 0.5$), 8 ($0.875$), 64 ($0.984375$) and the whole past ($a = 1$). Compared by angle (cosine), the viewer's setting, the vectors are normalised twice: each token's vector to unit length before the sum, so every token counts equally, and then the weighted past, $d = 1 - K_a(t,t')/\sqrt{K_a(t,t)K_a(t',t')}$. With lengths kept (the "dot" setting), louder vectors count more, and the distance is half the squared distance between the two weighted histories, scaled: $\big[(K_a(t,t) + K_a(t',t'))/2 - K_a(t,t')\big]/\bar K$, with $\bar K$ the mean of $K_a(t,t)$ over the analysed tokens. Three properties matter when reading results. Neighbours come in runs: $K(t,t')$ and $K(t+1,t'+1)$ share all but one term, so a chance agreement between two pasts persists along the alignment. At the whole past, pasts of different lengths are compared only over the shorter one: compared by angle, tokens at positions $t < t'$ can be at most $\sqrt{t/t'}$ similar, so neighbours sit at similar positions even for text with no structure; with lengths kept, the earliest analysed tokens end up close to everyone. And the recursion is computed exactly, in float64, never through a closed form that overflows.

What is removed, and which tokens are barred

Before comparing, an average hidden state is removed, so that what every token shares does not swamp the comparison. The main setting removes one average, taken over all {{fact:x.viewer.texts}} texts, and lets every other analysed token be a neighbour. Two kinds of contrast are also run: removing each text's own average instead, and barring either nearby tokens (same text, within 64 positions) or the token's whole own text. These contrasts are not corrections. A bar sets the barred share to zero by rule, so it says nothing about the model there.

Text-only inputs

To separate what the model computes from what repeated tokens and the comparison formula give on their own, the same pipeline is run on two inputs that know only which token is where. One gives each token id a fixed random vector. The other is each model's input embedding (layer 0), before any block runs. Where a model's neighbours match these, the pattern could have come from the text alone.

The random vectors are Gaussian, width 2,048, drawn once with a fixed seed, and the same at every position and in both models; they have no layers. Neither model adds a position embedding at the input, so layer 0 also depends only on the token id. Both inputs go through the same averaging, scaling and memory sum as the hidden states.

Ties

Real model states almost never tie. The text-only inputs do: with no memory, two copies of the same token are at distance exactly zero, so a frequent token can have hundreds of tied candidates.

Two candidates are tied if their distances differ by at most one part in $10^{12}$. The 64 neighbours are every candidate strictly closer than the 64th distance, plus tied candidates at the cutoff taken in (text, position) order, lowest text first. For the text-only inputs with no memory, this order partly reflects how the texts are numbered; the size of that effect was not measured. Overlaps between two neighbour lists are computed as the expected overlap under random tie-breaking, $\mathbb{E}|A \cap B| = \sum_j P(j \in A)\, P(j \in B)$. The rule for Stage B (below) instead uses the largest overlap the ties allow, so a candidate cannot pass by tie-breaking.

What was measured

Stage A computes no graph patterns. It makes four measurements on the stored hidden states. First, for every analysed token and every setting, its 64 nearest neighbours and where they sit: in another text, in the same text more than 64 positions away, or within 64 positions. Second, for sampled tokens near the end of each text, how the weight of the graph a later stage would have used is spread over those same three places. Third, on a fixed sample of tokens, whether neighbours share more of the model's next-token prediction than stand-ins do. Fourth, a rule written before any results, which used the graph weights and neighbour lists on those late tokens to choose a setting for Stage B. The owner later set its pick aside.

Every analysed token, every setting (2 models × 2 ways of comparing × 2 averages × 5 memories × 3 bar rules × 8 layers, plus the 2 text-only inputs): the shares of its 64 neighbours in other texts, in the same text more than 64 positions away, and within 64 positions on either side, each averaged over tokens; each token's median position gap to its same-text neighbours, averaged over tokens; the share whose current token is the same as the query's; and the overlap with the text-only inputs' lists and with the same setting's list at no memory. Chance levels are the shares among the tokens allowed as neighbours. (E1)

Graph weight on the last 512 positions. In the graph the original Stage B would have built, each token spreads a total weight of 1 over all other tokens, more to closer ones: $q(j \mid i) \propto \exp(-\beta_i (d_{ij} - \min_j d_{ij}))$, with $\beta_i$ set so that $1/\sum_j q(j \mid i)^2 = 64$. That is as concentrated as an even split over 64 tokens, yet much of the weight can still sit in a long, thin tail. For the sampled queries in the last 512 positions, against every token in the last 512 positions of every text, we record the weight on each of the three places; with nearby tokens barred, also the weight the unbarred row had put on them. Nothing $N \times N$ is stored and no eigenproblem is solved. (E3)

The prediction check on the query sample, with neighbours from layers 4, 8, 12 and 16. For a query and one of its neighbours, the prediction overlap is the probability both positions give to the same next tokens, within their top-20 lists. It is compared with the same overlap for a stand-in: another occurrence of the neighbour's token in the neighbour's text, not on the query's neighbour list, chosen at random. If the neighbour is in the query's text, the stand-in must also be at a similar distance from the query (1–8, 9–64, 65–512 or over 512 positions). The difference is the prediction advantage. Pairs fall into six groups, by where the neighbour sits and whether its token is frequent (at least 20 occurrences among the analysed tokens) or rare. A group counts only if at least 200 of its pairs, and at least half of them, found a stand-in; the score is the plain average over the groups that count. (E2)

The rule for Stage B, written before any results. Each of the {{fact:x.e3-selection.candidates}} candidate settings (memory 2, 8, 64 or whole × angle or lengths kept × the two averages × nearby tokens barred or not) is tested at each layer on four conditions: at least a quarter of its graph weight on other texts; not too much weight on nearby tokens; a neighbour list at most half the same as at no memory; and a list at most half the same as each text-only input's. A candidate passes if it meets all four at {{fact:x.e3-selection.required}} or more of the {{fact:x.e3-selection.sites}} layers, in both models. Passing candidates are ranked by fewest interventions first (one average and nothing barred, then one average with nearby tokens barred, then each text's own average, then that with nearby tokens barred), then angle before lengths kept, then the smallest overlap with the text-only inputs, then the shorter memory. The prediction advantage could add at most one extra example. The rule picked lengths kept (dot) at memory 64, with one average removed and nothing barred. The owner set this pick aside and redesigned Stage B (see Deferred).

Controls

Three kinds of check sit beside the real texts. Automated tests compared each step with slow direct versions and hand-worked cases before any money was spent. Spliced texts, made by cutting two documents into alternating segments, test whether neighbours follow their source document beyond what position alone gives. Synthetic texts with a planted hidden structure show what a known structure looks like as neighbours.

Tests before any spending (exp1/test_census.py): the memory sum as streamed, against an explicit feature map and a direct double loop; the three bar rules against allowed sets written by hand; planted exact ties at the cutoff, including 64 taken from 1,024 tied candidates against a set tying all 1,024 (expected overlap 0.0625, largest 1); stand-ins never equal to the query, its neighbour or another neighbour, and replayable from their seeds; statistics, document resampling and the spliced-text baseline against hand values.

Spliced texts with expected answers: two pairs of documents, each pair from two different sources, cut into alternating segments of 384 to 640 tokens or of 48 to 80 tokens, giving {{fact:x.viewer.stitched}} spliced texts. Each is analysed alone: its candidates are only its own tokens, and the one average of the {{fact:x.viewer.texts}} real texts is removed. The expectation: a token's neighbours should come from its own source more often than position alone explains, at little or no memory; at memory 64, less so for the short segments.

Synthetic texts with a planted hidden regime, a slow position drift or pure noise, run through the same census, so a known structure can be seen as neighbours. Only comparison by angle with one average removed was run on them ({{fact:x.e1-neighbours.synthetic.runs}} runs), not every combination of comparison and average.

Reference timescales

Two simple scales per layer, for reference beside the memory settings: how far back attention heads look, and how quickly hidden states change along a text. Neither measures how much context the model uses.

Per layer: how far back each attention head looks directly (its attention-weighted mean distance, leaving out attention to position 0; the median over heads), and the hidden-state half distance: the first lag at which the mean similarity (by angle) of two states of one text falls halfway from its lag-one value to the mean between texts. The half distance was measured at even layers only; odd layers were not measured, and every even layer reached halfway within 1,024 tokens. Neither is an effective context length. How to check there is nothing odd going on

An independent audit recomputes the results from the raw hidden states, with code that shares nothing with the pipeline. Its first report found a real bug: an integer overflow had zeroed the prediction overlap for neighbours and stand-ins in the later texts. The bug was fixed, the affected steps were re-run, and the audit then passed: {{fact:x.method.audit.checks}} checks, {{fact:x.method.audit.failed}} failed. The release uses the audited Stage A outputs, including the corrected prediction readout. The pilot (a separate local run) and the design settings are identified separately. You can also click any token in the viewer to see its neighbours, and a browser test checks the pages themselves.

An independent audit (exp1/audit_a.py: numpy only, importing nothing from the pipeline) takes {{fact:x.method.audit.rows}} audit queries. For each, it recomputes the complete distance row from the raw hidden states by the explicit step-by-step sum, then the bar rules, the 64 neighbours with ties, the graph weights and the weight removed by barring nearby tokens. It replays the stand-ins from their seeds. It recomputes the census statistics from the stored lists for a sample of tokens, the prediction check from its stored per-document sums, and the rule for Stage B. It also compares 1,000 random pairs per pass with values the census saved before it discarded its distance blocks. The site does not build as a release unless this report is present and passing.

The audit has failed once, for a real reason. Its first report on these data failed one check, readout.document_contributions, in every model and settings group: the prediction check's stored per-document sums did not match the audit's own recomputation. The cause was an int32 overflow in the pipeline (exp1/readout.py, the lookup key of the prediction overlap), which recorded zero overlap for any neighbour or stand-in in texts 24 to 31 and the end of text 23; the small test case could not reach those indices. The fix uses 64-bit keys and adds two regression tests; the prediction check, the rule for Stage B and the viewer export were re-run, and the audit then passed. Prediction advantages rose; top-1 match rates, the share of pairs with a stand-in and the rule's pick did not change. The release uses the audited outputs, including the corrected prediction readout (E2).

Deferred

Several extensions were left out of Stage A; they are listed below. The Stage B planned when Stage A ran has been replaced. After seeing Stage A's results, the owner set the rule's pick aside and asked for a new design, described at the end of this section. That design has been written up and reviewed, but none of it has been run.

  • Keeping only each token's nearest neighbours in the graph, instead of weights on every token: on a synthetic graph this was measured to shift the result (SPEC.md), so the planned graph kept every link.
  • Model-derived lag weights; a recurrent random sketch; attention-weighted links across layers; a training-checkpoint axis; a width sweep; bars that grow with memory; mixing angle and lengths-kept comparison; longer contexts; more models.
  • The owner's later list: a sentence-embedding or vector-database baseline on the same texts, and the Hankel predictive-state construction of Golowich, Liu and Shetty.

Stage B as now designed (SPEC_B.md) uses independent chunks instead of long texts. Each of 2,048 Pile test documents not used in Stage A is cut once at a random position. The model reads the document up to the cut, and the chunk is the last 64 tokens up to and including the cut. No two chunks share a document, so the chains of neighbours along one text seen in Stage A cannot form. One average is removed, chunks are compared by angle (the lengths-kept "dot" setting is deferred), and nothing is barred. A chunk is summarised three ways: its state at the cut (the main one), its recent past at memory 8, and the average of its 64 states. The model's next-token prediction at the cut is built into the graph, and the main test asks whether cross-layer patterns pay a smaller prediction-disagreement penalty with the correct chunk-prediction pairing than with shuffled pairings. If run, it would add a "Through depth" tab and entries E4 to E6. Nothing of it has been run.
